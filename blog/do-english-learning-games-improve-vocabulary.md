# Do English Learning Games Actually Improve Vocabulary?

*By [The Dobi Apps Team](https://dobiapps.com/authors/editorial) · Published 2026-09-13 · Originally published at [dobiapps.com](https://dobiapps.com/blog/do-english-learning-games-improve-vocabulary)*

> **A 2022 meta-analysis of 29 studies found game-based English vocabulary learning works short-term, but most of its edge over non-game study fades within weeks.**

**In short:** Game-based English vocabulary learning produces real short-term gains: a 2022 meta-analysis of 29 studies found a medium effect on immediate vocabulary tests. Most of that advantage over non-game instruction fades by a delayed test, though, so the mechanic matters more than the novelty: a game has to force repeated retrieval of the same word to leave a lasting effect.

## Do English Learning Games Actually Improve Vocabulary?

Digital games built around English vocabulary do produce measurable gains,
but the size of the gain depends heavily on what the game asks a learner to
do, not on whether it has points, levels, or badges attached. A 2022
meta-analysis of 29 studies found a medium-sized improvement in vocabulary
scores immediately after game-based practice ([Dixon, Dixon & Jordan,
2022](http://hdl.handle.net/10125/73464)). Much of that advantage over
non-game instruction disappears once learners are retested weeks later,
though. What survives is narrower: game mechanics that force a learner to
recall or produce a word, rather than simply recognize it, are the ones the
research ties to vocabulary gains that outlast the session.

That distinction, between a mechanic that forces real retrieval practice
and one that only decorates a session with rewards, is the difference this
post works through. A points counter or a level-up animation measures
engagement with the app, not acquisition of the word. The research below
looks specifically at what happens to vocabulary scores when the game
element is added, removed, or changed, which is a different question from
whether learners enjoy playing or keep opening the app.

## What Does the Research Actually Show About Game-Based Vocabulary Learning?

Two peer-reviewed meta-analyses, pooling dozens of individual experiments,
converge on the same broad conclusion: digital games produce a real,
medium-to-large vocabulary gain compared with non-game instruction, though
the exact size varies by how the comparison is set up.

Daniel Dixon, Tülay Dixon, and Eric Jordan analyzed 29 experimental studies
of digital game-based language learning published through January 2020
([Dixon, Dixon & Jordan, 2022](http://hdl.handle.net/10125/73464)). Between
groups, where one group of learners played a digital game and a comparable
group received non-game instruction, the weighted effect size was d = 0.50,
a small-to-medium advantage for the game group. Comparing the same learners
before and after playing, without a separate control group, produced a
larger effect of d = 0.95. An earlier, separate meta-analysis of 10 studies
by Meng-Hua Chen, Wen-Ta Tseng, and Tsung-Yuan Hsiao found a comparable
medium effect of d = 0.78 for digital games against traditional instruction
([Chen, Tseng & Hsiao, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.12526)).

> **d = 0.50** — weighted effect size of digital game-based learning on L2 vocabulary versus non-game instruction, across 21 between-groups studies (95% CI: 0.19 to 1.11)
>
> *Source: [Dixon, Dixon & Jordan, 2022](http://hdl.handle.net/10125/73464)*

Neither meta-analysis found that a game's genre or design mattered less
than whether it was marketed as an educational product. Chen, Tseng, and
Hsiao's moderator analysis found the effect on vocabulary varied
significantly with game design (Q = 5.857, p = 0.016) but not with learner
age (Q = 0.906, p = 0.341) or language background (Q = 0.0001, p = 0.994),
meaning the design of the game predicted the outcome far better than who
was playing it.

## Does the Vocabulary Boost From Games Last, or Does It Fade?

Most of a game's advantage over non-game instruction shrinks once
researchers test learners again weeks later, which is the single most
important caveat in this research. Dixon, Dixon, and Jordan's meta-analysis
compared delayed posttest scores between game-taught and conventionally
taught groups and found a weighted effect of only d = 0.09, a gap so small
its 95% confidence interval (-0.62 to 1.03) crosses zero ([Dixon, Dixon &
Jordan, 2022](http://hdl.handle.net/10125/73464)). Compared against their
own immediate posttest scores, the same game-taught learners lost ground:
the delayed-versus-immediate contrast was d = -0.69.

That pattern does not mean the games failed to teach anything. Compared
against their own pretest scores, the delayed posttest still showed a large
effect (d = 1.36), so a real share of new vocabulary survived. What
disappeared was the game group's edge over the group that studied without a
game, not the vocabulary itself. Applied linguist Jonathon Reinhardt has
raised a related concern about apps designed specifically as language-learning
games: they risk becoming "repetitive and superficial tasks in which the
learning objectives are too obvious," which quietly reduces the retrieval
challenge that makes the practice stick (Reinhardt, 2019, as quoted in
[Dixon, Dixon & Jordan, 2022](http://hdl.handle.net/10125/73464), p. 2).

The practical reading is not that games stop working after one session. It
is that the extra motivation a game supplies has to translate into more
retrieval practice, not just more time in the app, or the vocabulary gain
converges toward whatever a learner would have retained without the game at
all.

## Do Points, Levels, and Badges Teach Vocabulary by Themselves?

Points, levels, and badges are motivation tools, not vocabulary-teaching
mechanisms, and the research on general gamification treats them that way.
A peer-reviewed 2020 systematic review of 46 empirical studies on
gamification in education found consistent evidence that game elements raise motivation and
engagement, but flagged the link between specific game mechanics and actual
learning outcomes as inconsistent across studies, varying with duration,
task difficulty, and how the mechanic was implemented ([Zainuddin, Chu,
Shujahat & Perera, 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X19301058)).
In other words, a badge reliably makes a learner want to keep going. Whether
that extra time in the app produces vocabulary a learner did not have
before depends entirely on what the badge was awarded for.

A badge earned for opening the app every day rewards the same behavior a
plain streak counter rewards, covered separately in [how to build an
English learning habit that lasts](https://dobiapps.com/blog/building-daily-english-learning-habit).
A badge earned for correctly recalling ten new words in a row rewards
something closer to the retrieval practice the vocabulary meta-analyses
above measured. Both use identical UI: a counter, an animation, a small
reward. Only one of them is doing anything a vocabulary test would detect.

This is the practical filter a learner or a parent can apply to any English
learning game inside an app: ask what specific action triggers the reward,
not how satisfying the reward feels. An app that cannot answer that question
clearly is optimizing for session length, which is a legitimate business
goal but a different one from building vocabulary. The same filter applies
to a game built around comics or short video clips rather than text, since
[comics and graphic novels](https://dobiapps.com/blog/learning-english-through-comics-graphic-novels)
and [captioned video](https://dobiapps.com/blog/subtitles-captioned-video-english-vocabulary)
can supply the same context-driven word encounters a narrative game does.

## Which Game Design Choices Produce Bigger Vocabulary Gains?

Game genre and structure move the size of the vocabulary effect more than
almost any other variable the research has tested, including a game's
educational branding. The comparison below pulls the moderator results
reported across the two meta-analyses discussed above.

| Comparison | Studies (k) | Effect size (d) | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Digital game vs. non-game instruction, immediate posttest (between-groups) | 21 | 0.50 | Small-to-medium vocabulary edge for the game group right after the study |
| Same learners, before vs. after playing (within-group) | 29 | 0.95 | Medium gain from pretest to posttest |
| Digital game vs. non-game instruction, delayed posttest (between-groups) | 8 | 0.09 (CI crosses zero) | The game group's edge is no longer distinguishable from none, weeks later |
| Adventure-genre games vs. non-adventure games, vocabulary outcomes | 10 | 1.867 vs. 0.705 | Narrative, task-driven games produced a much larger effect than drill-style games |
| Games with teacher-provided supplementary material vs. none | 7 vs. 22 (within-group) | 2.09 vs. 1.05 | Adding a teacher check or worksheet alongside a game roughly doubled the effect |

Games built around adventure or narrative tasks, where a word has to be
understood or produced to progress through a story, produced roughly
double the vocabulary effect of games without that structure ([Dixon, Dixon
& Jordan, 2022](http://hdl.handle.net/10125/73464), citing [Chen, Tseng &
Hsiao, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.12526)). Supplementary material,
such as a teacher reviewing what a learner got wrong in the game, also
roughly doubled the measured effect. Games designed specifically for
entertainment rather than for language instruction performed at least as
well as, and in some comparisons better than, games built explicitly for L2
education, though the entertainment-game studies varied widely enough that
the two categories' confidence intervals still overlap.

## How Should You Judge Whether a Learning Game Is Actually Building Vocabulary?

A learner evaluating a vocabulary game inside an English learning app can
apply the same distinctions the research above draws between a strong and
a weak design, without needing to read the studies themselves.

| Signal | Associated with a real vocabulary gain | Associated with a weaker or fading effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What advancing the game requires | Recalling or producing the word to proceed | Recognizing or tapping the word passively |
| How the reward is triggered | Correct retrieval of a specific word | Time spent, session completed, or login streak |
| Whether a person checks the output | A teacher, parent, or built-in review of mistakes | No review beyond the game's own scoring |
| What the game is built around | A story or task where the word carries consequence | An isolated drill with no surrounding context |
| Whether progress is retested later | Vocabulary reappears in a later session for review | Each word is scored once and never revisited |

None of these signals require special expertise to check. Opening a
vocabulary game inside an app and asking "what did I just have to do to
earn that reward" answers the first two rows directly. [Storybook, from
Dobi Apps](https://dobiapps.com/storybook), builds its reading games around this principle: its
[App Store
listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/story-book-english/id6498920336)
describes rewards, levels, and achievements that unlock as a learner
progresses through actual books, comics, and stories, tying the game layer
to material a learner is already reading rather than to a stand-alone drill.
A learner who wants the same underlying vocabulary-in-context principle in
audio form can pair [Storybook](https://dobiapps.com/storybook) with [Dobi Apps'
Audiobook](https://dobiapps.com/audiobook), which organizes its library by CEFR level from A1
to C2. A game that skips level matching entirely, throwing the same
difficulty at every player, works against the same [comprehensible
input](https://dobiapps.com/blog/comprehensible-input-english-reading-level) principle that
makes leveled reading effective: a word surrounded by too many other
unfamiliar words gives a player nothing to guess its meaning from, game or
no game.

## How Do Vocabulary Games Fit Alongside Spaced Repetition and Reading?

A vocabulary game and a spaced repetition system solve two different parts
of the same problem, and neither one replaces the other. A game is
typically where a learner first meets a word inside some task with a
consequence attached: choose the wrong word and a character in the story
does not progress, or a puzzle does not solve. That first, engaging
encounter is exactly what the moderate-to-large effect sizes in the
research above are measuring.

What a game rarely does well is bring that same word back for review at the
moment it is about to be forgotten. That scheduling problem is what [spaced
repetition](https://dobiapps.com/blog/spaced-repetition-english-vocabulary) is built to solve,
reviewing a word at lengthening intervals rather than scoring it once and
moving on. A learner who wants both benefits gets more from combining a game
that introduces new words in context with a spaced review system that
brings them back on a schedule than from expecting either tool to do the
other's job. The same logic applies to [extensive reading through
stories](https://dobiapps.com/blog/learn-english-through-stories): a story supplies repeated,
low-stakes exposure to a word across different contexts, which a points
system inside a reading app can track and reward without being the actual
mechanism doing the teaching.

A learner comparing tools across a broader set of goals, not vocabulary
alone, can start from [the top 5 apps for learning English in
2026](https://dobiapps.com/blog/top-5-apps-for-learning-english), which covers where a
gamified app such as Duolingo fits next to a story-based app, a
pronunciation coach, and a spaced repetition tool. Rereading a familiar
story or replaying a slower audiobook chapter are two other low-effort ways
to get the same repeated exposure a game provides, without a single point
awarded for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do game-based English apps work equally well for children and adults?

The evidence available says yes. A 2018 meta-analysis of digital game-based vocabulary learning found the effect on vocabulary gains did not vary significantly by learner age, only by the design of the game itself. A well-designed game appears to help a learner at almost any age; a poorly designed one does not, regardless of who is playing it.

### Do Duolingo's streaks, points, and leaderboards build vocabulary by themselves?

Not on their own. Those mechanics are built to keep a learner returning to the app, which is a habit-formation problem, not a vocabulary-acquisition one. A 12-week study of adult beginners studying Turkish on Duolingo, reported in Dixon, Dixon, and Jordan's 2022 meta-analysis, found only one of nine participants scored 70% or higher on their university's first-semester exam, a result the original researchers linked to the app's grammar-translation-style exercises rather than to its streak or point system.

### Should a vocabulary game replace spaced repetition flashcards?

No, the two do different jobs. A vocabulary game is usually where a word is first met and practiced in a task with some consequence attached. Spaced repetition is the schedule that brings a word back for review right before it would otherwise be forgotten. Games are good at the first job and weak at the second, since most award points for the current session rather than tracking what needs review weeks later.
