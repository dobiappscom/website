# Do Comics and Graphic Novels Help You Learn English?

*By [The Dobi Apps Team](https://dobiapps.com/authors/editorial) · Published 2026-09-11 · Originally published at [dobiapps.com](https://dobiapps.com/blog/learning-english-through-comics-graphic-novels)*

> **Comics pair pictures with text, and research on that combination found EFL learners scored 45% higher after multimodal study than after text-only study.**

**In short:** Comics and graphic novels help learn English because they pair text with pictures, and pictures give context for guessing an unfamiliar word's meaning. A controlled study of English learners found a 45% higher score after studying with text-plus-picture material than with text alone, and a separate study found a graphic-novel group outperformed a text-only control group on reading comprehension.

## Why Do Comics Work for Learning English?

Comics combine written text with pictures in the same panel, and that
pairing gives a reader two sources of meaning instead of one. A word a
reader does not know can often be worked out from what the picture shows,
the same guess-from-context mechanism that makes [comprehensible
input](https://dobiapps.com/blog/comprehensible-input-english-reading-level) effective, but with
a visual clue standing in for some of the surrounding sentences a prose text
would otherwise need to supply that context.

This combination has a name in reading research: multimodal input, meaning
information delivered through more than one channel at once, in this case
the verbal and the visual. Comics are not the only multimodal format, audio
paired with text is another example covered in [how reading while listening
to audiobooks builds English listening
fluency](https://dobiapps.com/blog/reading-while-listening-english-fluency), but comics are one
of the few formats where the second channel is present on every single page
rather than running in the background.

Comics also compress dialogue. A comic page fits far fewer words per panel
than a prose page fits per paragraph, which pushes writers toward shorter,
more direct sentences and toward idiomatic, spoken-sounding English rather
than the more formal register a textbook passage tends to use. That gives a
reader repeated exposure to natural spoken patterns in a format that a
picture already helps make sense of.

## What Does Research Say About Comics and English Reading Comprehension?

Reema Aldahash and Sultan Altalhab tested graphic novels directly with 66
intermediate English learners in Saudi Arabia, split into an experimental
group that read graphic novels and a control group that did not. Their 2020
study, published in the International Journal of Applied Linguistics and
English Literature, reports that reading graphic novels "positively affects
reading comprehension as the experimental group achieved superior results
to the control group," a result stated as a group comparison rather than
with a specific percentage.

A separate 2022 study in Frontiers in Psychology measured the broader
multimodal mechanism comics rely on, text paired with pictures, more
precisely, though the study itself used English phrase-learning material
rather than comics specifically.

> **45%** — higher posttest score for English learners who studied phrases with multimodal text-plus-picture material (70.48) versus text-only material (48.53), among 66 EFL learners (p < .001)
>
> *Source: [Huang et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9172966/)*

Both groups started from a statistically indistinguishable pretest score, so
the gap that opened up by the posttest tracks the study material each group
used rather than a difference the groups started with. The two studies
point at the same underlying effect from different angles: adding a visual
channel to text measurably helps English learners retain what they read, and
comics are a text format built around exactly that pairing on every page.

## Do You Need Background Knowledge to Read Comics in English?

Comics carry their own conventions, and a reader unfamiliar with them can
struggle with a comic despite having no trouble with the English inside it.
John D. Benjamin's 2021 study in Second Language Research & Practice
surveyed 26 advanced L2 learners and found that more than a quarter had
never read comics at all, in any language, before the study.

Panel order, the distinction between a speech balloon and a thought balloon,
and which direction to read a page in are learned skills rather than
instinct, and a reader who has never encountered them can lose track of a
comic's story for reasons that have nothing to do with vocabulary. Once
learners in Benjamin's study did engage with a comic, the response was
strongly positive:

> “Most participants also considered reading comics as a useful way to learn a language.”
>
> — [John D. Benjamin, 2021](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/69856/1/02_01_10125-69856.pdf)

Of the participants who found comics well suited to language learning, 68.1%
specifically pointed to pictures as help for understanding new vocabulary in
context. None of the 26 participants said comics were not useful for
learning a language. The practical takeaway for a new reader is small:
spend the first few pages of an unfamiliar comic noticing panel order and
balloon shapes before worrying about difficulty level, since that overhead
disappears after the first title.

## How Do Comics Compare to Text-Only Graded Readers for Learning English?

Comics and text-only graded readers both deliver comprehensible input, but
they trade off differently on what makes a book approachable at a given
level.

| | Text-only graded reader | Comics and graphic novels |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Context for unknown words | Surrounding sentences only | Pictures plus surrounding dialogue |
| Vocabulary per page | Higher, full sentences | Lower, compressed dialogue |
| Register | Often more formal or narrative | Often closer to spoken, idiomatic English |
| Preparation needed | None beyond reading level | Panel order and balloon conventions, once |
| Best fit | Learners comfortable with dense text | Beginners, reluctant readers, and visual learners |

Neither format replaces the other, and the two are frequently combined by
readers who use one to build reading stamina and the other to keep dialogue
and idioms fresh. A learner working through [how to learn English
effectively through stories](https://dobiapps.com/blog/learn-english-through-stories) with
text-only graded readers can add comics on days when motivation to sit with
dense text runs low, without losing the extensive reading habit that method
depends on. New words that turn up in either format are worth the same
follow-up review, covered in [how spaced repetition builds English
vocabulary retention](https://dobiapps.com/blog/spaced-repetition-english-vocabulary).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are comics too simple to actually build English vocabulary?

No. Comics compress dialogue into short lines, which forces more precise word choice rather than simpler word choice, and idioms and colloquial speech appear more densely in comic dialogue than in a comparable page of prose.

### Do beginners need any preparation before reading English comics?

A short one, if the reader has never read comics before: panel order, speech balloons versus thought balloons, and where to look next on a page are conventions rather than instinct. A research study found more than a quarter of participants had never read comics at all before the conventions were explained.
