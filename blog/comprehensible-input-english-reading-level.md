# Comprehensible Input: Find Your Right English Level

*By [The Dobi Apps Team](https://dobiapps.com/authors/editorial) · Published 2026-09-11 · Updated 2026-09-16 · Originally published at [dobiapps.com](https://dobiapps.com/blog/comprehensible-input-english-reading-level)*

> **Comprehensible input means text you can almost fully understand. A landmark 2000 study found 98% word coverage is the threshold, and how to test your own level.**

**In short:** Comprehensible input is language just beyond a learner's current level: understandable, but still slightly challenging. A study of 66 English learners found comprehension collapsed once unfamiliar words passed a threshold, and that roughly 98% vocabulary coverage was needed to gain comprehension without help. Matching material to that level is what makes reading and listening practice actually build fluency.

![The Extensive Reading Foundation's graded reader scale, mapping reader levels against headwords and CEFR bands](https://erfoundation.org/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/ERF_Scale_2013-800.png)

*Hero image: [The Extensive Reading Foundation](https://erfoundation.org/wordpress/graded-readers/erf-graded-reader-scale/).*

## What Is Comprehensible Input in Language Learning?

Comprehensible input is language a learner can understand well enough to
follow, while it still contains something slightly beyond what they already
know. Applied linguist Stephen Krashen described the target with a short
formula, i+1: "i" stands for a learner's current level of competence, and "+1"
is the small stretch of new language just above it, not five levels above it.

The mechanism behind comprehensible input is straightforward. A learner who
understands almost everything in a text can use the surrounding context to
work out the meaning of the few unfamiliar words or grammar patterns left.
Text that sits far above a learner's level removes that context, so there is
nothing left to guess from. Text that sits at or below a learner's level
removes the unfamiliar part entirely, so there is nothing new to acquire.

Comprehensible input is not tied to one medium. A graded reader, a leveled
audiobook, a conversation with a patient speaker, and a [subtitled
video](https://dobiapps.com/blog/subtitles-captioned-video-english-vocabulary) can all deliver
it, provided the language stays inside a learner's comprehension range. Extensive reading through graded stories, covered in [how to learn
English effectively through stories](https://dobiapps.com/blog/learn-english-through-stories),
is one of the best-studied ways to supply comprehensible input consistently.
What changes between formats is how a learner locates material at the right
level in the first place, which is the practical problem the rest of this
post works through.

## How Much of a Text Do You Need to Understand for Comprehensible Input to Work?

Reading comprehension does not decline gradually as unfamiliar words
increase. It drops sharply once the density of unknown words passes a
specific point, and researchers have measured where that point sits.

Marcella Hu Hsueh-chao and Paul Nation, both at Victoria University of
Wellington, tested this directly in a study of 66 pre-university English
learners. Each learner read one of four versions of the same 673-word fiction
story, with 100%, 95%, 90%, or 80% of the running words left as familiar
vocabulary and the rest replaced with nonsense words of matching frequency.
Every learner then took a 14-item multiple-choice comprehension test.

> **98%** — approximate vocabulary coverage Hu and Nation (2000) found necessary for a learner to gain unassisted comprehension of a fiction text, based on 66 pre-university EFL learners
>
> *Source: [Hu & Nation, 2000](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/43)*

The comprehension gap between coverage levels was large enough to see in the
raw scores, not only in a statistical model:

| Vocabulary coverage | Learners tested | Average score (out of 14) | Learners reaching adequate comprehension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100% | 17 | 12.24 | 15 of 17 |
| 95% | 17 | 10.18 | 6 of 17 |
| 90% | 16 | 9.50 | 4 of 16 |
| 80% | 16 | 6.06 | 0 of 16 |

> “It seems that around 98% coverage of vocabulary is needed for learners to gain unassisted comprehension of a fiction text.”
>
> — [Hu Hsueh-chao and Nation, 2000](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/43)

A 2023 replication by Benjamin Kremmel, published in the journal Language
Learning, tested the same question with 104 adult English learners in Sri
Lanka rather than the original New Zealand classroom, and could not fully
reproduce the 98% figure. The result does not overturn the core finding, that
comprehension drops sharply once unfamiliar words pass a threshold; it shows
that the exact percentage shifts somewhat with the learner population and
setting. Treat 98% as a strong working target rather than a fixed line.

## How Do You Find Your Right English Reading Level?

A learner does not need a vocabulary test to estimate whether a text sits at
their comprehensible input level. The Extensive Reading Foundation recommends
a simple self-check before starting any book: can you read it without a
dictionary, are you reading it at a natural pace, and do you understand
almost everything. Three "yes" answers mean the level fits; any "no" means
the text is too hard for comfortable comprehensible input.

A quick version of the same test works on a single page. Open a random page
in the middle of the book, not the first page, since openings are sometimes
written easier than the rest. Count the words that would need a dictionary
lookup. Under Hu and Nation's data above, more than one or two unfamiliar
words per page of ordinary length already pushes coverage below the 95% mark
where most learners stopped reaching adequate comprehension.

Graded readers, ebooks, and audiobooks organized by the CEFR scale, from A1
for beginners through C2 for near-native learners, remove most of the
guesswork by pre-sorting material into bands. A learner still has to confirm
the assigned level fits with the page test above, since two publishers can
label the same difficulty differently, but starting from a leveled library
means each book is already close to the right range rather than a random
pick from a general bookstore shelf. A learner who has not yet placed
themselves on that scale can start with [how to find your CEFR English
level](https://dobiapps.com/blog/find-your-cefr-english-level), which covers free tests and a
self-assessment checklist for doing exactly that. A learner who also wants a
broader set of tools to compare, beyond reading level alone, can start from
[the top 5 apps for learning English](https://dobiapps.com/blog/top-5-apps-for-learning-english).

## How Do Leveled Audiobooks and Ebooks Make Comprehensible Input Easier to Get?

book. A library organized by level does that filtering before a learner opens

## What Happens When Reading Material Is Too Hard or Too Easy?

Material above a learner's comprehensible input level, i+10 rather than i+1,
produces the same experience as the 80% coverage group in Hu and Nation's
data: constant dictionary lookups, no coverage-driven comprehension, and no
functioning source of context to learn new words from. None of the learners
reading the 80% version reached adequate comprehension on the test, and the
same frustration shows up in a real book long before a learner reaches page
two.

Material below a learner's level carries a quieter cost. Nothing in the text
is unfamiliar, so there is no i+1 left to acquire, and a learner who wanted a
gentle read gets no vocabulary growth in exchange for the time spent reading.
The fix in both directions is the same page test described above, applied
again whenever a book stops feeling right partway through.

## How Do You Know When to Move Up a Level?

The same page test that finds a starting level also signals when to leave
it. Once a book at the current level rarely produces an unfamiliar word, the
text has moved from i+1 toward i+0: comprehensible, but no longer supplying
much new language to acquire. That is the point to try a book one level up
and run the dictionary-lookup count again.

Moving up too early undoes the benefit this whole approach is built on.
Comprehension has to stay high for the guess-from-context mechanism to keep
working, so a learner who jumps two levels at once, chasing faster progress,
lands back in the 80%-coverage condition where comprehension breaks down
instead of building. Progress through comprehensible input comes from
staying at 95 to 98% coverage and moving the target text up gradually as
vocabulary grows, the same incremental process extensive reading relies on in
[how to learn English effectively through stories](https://dobiapps.com/blog/learn-english-through-stories).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is comprehensible input the same thing as extensive reading?

No. Extensive reading is one way to deliver comprehensible input, through books read for pleasure. Comprehensible input is the broader condition, language a learner can mostly understand, and it applies to listening and audiobooks just as much as to reading.

### Can comprehensible input work through listening alone, without reading?

Yes, as long as the audio is understandable. Reading the text at the same time makes the input easier to process for a learner who is still building listening fluency, which is why pairing a leveled audiobook with its text is a common way to apply the same idea.
