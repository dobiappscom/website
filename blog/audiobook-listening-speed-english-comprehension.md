# Does Audiobook Playback Speed Affect English Comprehension?

*By [The Dobi Apps Team](https://dobiapps.com/authors/editorial) · Published 2026-09-13 · Updated 2026-09-14 · Originally published at [dobiapps.com](https://dobiapps.com/blog/audiobook-listening-speed-english-comprehension)*

> **A 1997 study found slowing audio down improved L2 listening comprehension, and a 2024 study of 168 learners found speed interacts with proficiency level.**

**In short:** Adjusting audiobook playback speed changes English listening comprehension in both directions. A 1997 study found comprehension improved when learners slowed audio down, and a 2024 study of 168 learners found playback speed interacts with proficiency level. The right starting speed depends on a learner's CEFR level, not on defaulting to 1.0x.

## Does Changing Audiobook Playback Speed Change English Listening Comprehension?

Yes. Audiobook playback speed measurably changes how much a second-language
listener understands, and the effect runs in both directions. A 1997 study in
Applied Linguistics found that when English learners were given control over a
recording's speech rate, their listening comprehension improved, and the
improvement came specifically from slowing the recording down (Zhao, 1997). A
2024 study in the International Multilingual Research Journal tested three
fixed speeds, 0.75x, 1.0x, and 1.25x, on 168 multilingual English learners
listening to a TED talk, and found that both playback speed and a learner's
existing proficiency level affected scores on comprehension questions (Zong,
Polat, & Mahalingappa, 2024).

Neither study treats speed as a minor setting. Zhao's learners were not simply
stating a preference: their test scores changed measurably once they exercised
control over the recording, primarily by slowing audio down. Zong, Polat, and
Mahalingappa's three-speed design shows the same variable working in the other
direction. Speeding audio up interacts with proficiency, so the same 1.25x
setting that a C1 listener shrugs off can cost a lower-intermediate listener
real comprehension points.

Audiobook playback speed is not a cosmetic feature bolted onto an app. It is
one of the few variables a learner can control directly, session by session,
without needing different source material at all. The rest of this guide
covers where to start, when to change that setting, and why the number
labeled "1.0x" is not automatically the right one to leave it on.

## Does Slowing Down Audiobook Playback Help English Listening Comprehension?

Slowing an audiobook down improves comprehension for English learners,
according to the clearest direct evidence on the question. Yong Zhao's 1997
study in Applied Linguistics gave second-language learners control over a
recording's speech rate while they listened, rather than fixing the rate in
advance the way most listening tests do. Zhao reports that "when given
control, students' listening comprehension improved" and that the improvement
"was achieved by slowing down the speech rate," not by leaving it unchanged.

That detail matters for anyone deciding how to use an audiobook app's speed
control today. Zhao's learners were not simply told a slower recording would
help and then tested regardless. They were free to leave the rate untouched,
and the ones whose comprehension improved were the ones who chose to slow the
audio down themselves. Zhao's study compared individual listeners against
their own baseline rather than only comparing group averages, which is part of
why the result is treated as a foundational finding in this area rather than a
single small-sample result.

The practical read is straightforward. A slower playback speed is not a crutch
a confident learner should feel embarrassed about using. For a learner whose
goal in a given session is following an audiobook's plot or argument, not
building tolerance for native speed, slowing the recording down is a
legitimate, evidence-backed way to raise how much of that session actually
lands.

## What Happens When You Speed Up Audiobook Playback Instead?

Speeding audiobook playback up does not affect every learner equally, and the
effect depends heavily on existing proficiency. Jie Zong, Nihat Polat, and
Laura Mahalingappa's 2024 study, published in the International Multilingual
Research Journal, tested 168 multilingual English learners at English-medium
institutions in Turkey and China, playing the same 10-minute TED talk at three
fixed speeds: 0.75x, the default 1.0x, and 1.25x. Learners then answered a mix
of easier and harder comprehension questions, while the researchers also
measured overall English proficiency and listening sub-scores.

> **168 learners, 3 speeds** — tested at 0.75x, 1.0x, and 1.25x playback on a TED-talk listening test; both speed and English proficiency significantly affected comprehension scores
>
> *Source: [Zong, Polat & Mahalingappa, 2024, International Multilingual Research Journal](https://doi.org/10.1080/19313152.2024.2362463)*

The study's authors report that playback speed and proficiency level "matter
both for the easier and harder comprehension questions," which means a single
fixed speed setting does not serve every listener, or every level in an app's
catalog, equally well. A learner near the top of the proficiency range in that
study had more room to absorb a faster playback speed without losing
comprehension. A learner nearer the bottom lost more ground at 1.25x than a
stronger listener did at the same setting.

The practical takeaway is not that speeding up is always risky. It is that
speeding up is a decision to make against a learner's current proficiency
level, not a setting to leave permanently on an app's own default just because
it plays automatically.

## Why Isn't 1.0x Playback Speed Automatically the Right Speed?

The "1.0x" label on a playback speed control is not a scientifically
calibrated optimum. It is simply the pace at which a narrator happened to
read a given recording, and English audiobook narration is typically recorded
close to Audible's own stated rate of 155 words per minute, or 9,300 words per
finished hour. That pace was set for intelligibility to a general audience,
not tuned to any individual learner's real-time processing speed.

Two separate pieces of evidence point at this conclusion from opposite
directions. On the fast side, a 2021 study in the Journal of Experimental
Psychology: Human Perception and Performance found that fluent English
listeners' most efficient listening rate, the fastest speed at which
comprehension stayed high, averaged around 269 words per minute before
dropping off sharply by 315 words per minute (Kuperman et al., 2021). That
means even a fluent listener is not being shortchanged by speeding a
155-words-per-minute recording up somewhat. There is headroom above a
narrator's natural pace before comprehension actually suffers, at least for a
proficient listener.

On the slow side, Zhao's 1997 finding and the Zong, Polat, and Mahalingappa
2024 study both show a fixed 1.0x setting under-serving a learner whose
real-time processing speed sits below the narrator's natural pace. Put
together, 1.0x is a narrator's default, not a listener's optimum in either
direction. The right playback speed depends on the gap between narration pace
and a given learner's current processing speed, not on the number printed on
the button.

## What Playback Speed Should You Start At by CEFR Level?

The CEFR's own listening descriptors give a level-by-level picture of how
much unmodified speech a learner can follow, and that picture lines up with a
practical starting point for audiobook playback speed. The self-assessment
grid published by the Council of Europe and Europass describes an A1 listener
as needing people to "speak slowly and clearly," a B1 listener as needing
delivery that is "relatively slow and clear," and a C2 listener as having "no
difficulty...even when delivered at fast native speed." Those descriptors are
not playback-speed research on their own, but they describe the same gap in
processing speed that the Zhao (1997) and Zong, Polat, and Mahalingappa (2024)
studies measured directly.

| CEFR level | CEFR listening descriptor (Council of Europe / Europass) | Reasonable starting playback speed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1-A2 | "when people speak slowly and clearly" | 0.6x-0.75x |
| B1 | "when the delivery is relatively slow and clear" | 0.75x-0.85x |
| B2 | "extended speech...provided the topic is reasonably familiar" | 0.85x-1.0x |
| C1 | "even when it is not clearly structured" | 1.0x-1.1x |
| C2 | "even when delivered at fast native speed" | 1.0x-1.25x |

These ranges are a starting point to test against a learner's own
comprehension, not a fixed prescription lifted from a study that measured
each level separately. A B1 learner who finds 0.85x still leaves them lost on
every third sentence should drop further. A C1 learner who finds 1.0x
effortless has room to move toward the 1.25x speed that Zong, Polat, and
Mahalingappa tested as their fastest condition, without expecting the same
comprehension cost a lower-proficiency listener would pay at that speed.
Matching playback speed to a level only works alongside picking material at
the right vocabulary level in the first place, covered in [comprehensible
input and finding your right English reading
level](https://dobiapps.com/blog/comprehensible-input-english-reading-level).

## When and How Should You Increase Your Playback Speed?

Increasing playback speed works best as a small, repeated adjustment tied to
a comprehension check, not a single jump to native pace. A practical rule:
once a learner can follow an audiobook chapter at a given speed and correctly
answer basic content questions about it, without replaying sentences, across
two or three separate sessions, that speed has room to move up. A step of
0.05x to 0.1x at a time keeps the increase small enough that comprehension
does not collapse the way it did for lower-proficiency listeners at the
fastest setting in Zong, Polat, and Mahalingappa's 2024 study.

Multi-day, incremental training also has direct support from research on
time-compressed speech. A 2012 study in PLoS One by Karen Banai and Yishai
Lavner trained non-native listeners on artificially sped-up speech across
five sessions, roughly 300 sentences per session, with the compression rate
adjusted automatically based on each listener's performance rather than fixed
at one setting. Banai and Lavner report that "trained listeners improved
significantly more than untrained listeners on the trained condition" and
that the improvement "generalized...to two untrained conditions in which
different talkers presented the trained materials."

That generalization is the practical point. Training was not tied to
memorizing one specific recording; gains transferred to new sentences and new
speakers, which is what a learner actually needs from faster playback
practice. A learner increasing speed gradually, session over session, rather
than all at once, is following the same adaptive structure that produced a
generalizable result in Banai and Lavner's study, and building the habit
consistently matters as much as the increments themselves, covered in [how to
build an English learning habit that
lasts](https://dobiapps.com/blog/building-daily-english-learning-habit).

## Should You Optimize for Comprehension Now or Train Your Ear for Native Speed Later?

Maximizing comprehension in a single session and training an ear for native
playback speed are different goals, and a learner has to decide which one a
given session is for. Slowing an audiobook down, the intervention Zhao's 1997
study measured, raises how much of that session's content actually gets
understood right now. It does not, by itself, build the ability to
understand a native speaker talking at a normal pace with no playback control
available at all, the situation a learner faces in an actual conversation.

Training tolerance for faster speech is a separate, slower project, and the
evidence for it comes from a different kind of study than the comprehension
studies above. Banai and Lavner's 2012 training study needed five sessions of
deliberate practice before its non-native listeners showed a generalizable
gain, not one lucky exposure to fast speech. A learner who only ever listens
at 0.7x is optimizing for today's comprehension at the cost of ever building
tolerance for the roughly 155-words-per-minute pace most native narration and
conversation actually run at.

Neither goal is wrong on its own, but they call for different playback
habits. A learner cramming for a listening exam next week, or simply trying
to enjoy a specific audiobook's plot, should lower the speed and prioritize
comprehension now. A learner with months of runway, working toward following
native-speed conversation with no speed control available, should treat
higher-speed sessions as deliberate practice, the way Banai and Lavner's
participants did, even when a slower setting would feel easier in the moment.
The same incremental logic is what makes [spaced repetition build English
vocabulary retention](https://dobiapps.com/blog/spaced-repetition-english-vocabulary): small,
repeated challenges beat one comfortable routine repeated forever.

## How Is Adjusting Playback Speed Different From Shadowing or Reading While Listening?

Adjusting playback speed changes how fast audio plays. It does not add
speaking or a second input channel the way two related techniques do.
Shadowing means speaking an audiobook's words out loud in real time, matching
the narrator's rhythm and intonation, a different skill from passively
listening at a slower or faster setting, covered in [does shadowing
audiobooks improve English
pronunciation](https://dobiapps.com/blog/shadowing-audiobooks-english-pronunciation). Reading
while listening means following printed text with your eyes while the same
audio plays, adding a visual channel rather than changing the audio's pace at
all, covered in [does reading while listening to audiobooks improve English
fluency](https://dobiapps.com/blog/reading-while-listening-english-fluency).

These three techniques are not competitors for the same slot in a study
routine. A learner can read while listening at a slowed-down speed to make an
unfamiliar chapter followable, then shadow a short passage from the same
chapter once its content is familiar, at whatever speed makes the words
reproducible out loud. Captions can serve a similar supporting role at a
faster speed, covered in [do subtitles help you learn English
vocabulary](https://dobiapps.com/blog/subtitles-captioned-video-english-vocabulary). Playback
speed is a dial that applies underneath any of those techniques, or
underneath plain listening with no text and no speaking at all, rather than a
fourth technique competing against them for attention.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does slowing down audiobook playback count as cheating when learning English?

No. A 1997 study found listening comprehension improved specifically because learners chose to slow audio down when given control over its speed, not despite doing so. Treating the slow-down control as a legitimate tool rather than a shortcut to avoid matches what the research found.

### Is there one best playback speed for every English learner?

No. A 2024 study of 168 multilingual learners found that both playback speed and proficiency level affected comprehension scores, so the same speed setting helps one learner and costs another. Starting near the CEFR-based ranges in this guide and adjusting from your own comprehension works better than copying someone else's setting.
